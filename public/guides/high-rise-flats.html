Living in a high-rise flat: power cuts, water and fire

6 minute read · Last checked 12 November 2026 ·

Most emergency advice is written for houses. If you live in a block, a power cut changes more than the lights. Here's what's different, and what to sort out while things are normal.

What stops when the power goes

  • The lifts. Plan as if you'll be using the stairs, maybe for days. If anyone in your home couldn't manage the stairs, see "If someone can't use the stairs" below.
  • The water, on higher floors. Many blocks pump water up to the top floors. When the pumps stop, taps upstairs can run dry quickly, even though the water main is fine. Keep bottled water in the flat, and fill bottles and pans as soon as you're warned of a cut.
  • The door entry. Buzzers, fobs and keypads need power. Depending on the system, the main door may lock shut or be left open. Carers, nurses and family may not be able to get in, and you may not be able to buzz anyone up. Find out now what your building's door does, and agree a plan with anyone who visits regularly.
  • Corridor and stair lighting. Emergency lights only run for a limited time on batteries. Keep a head torch by the door, so both hands are free on the stairs.
  • Electric hob and heating. Most flats have no gas. You'll need food that doesn't need cooking, and a plan for keeping warm.

Never cook or heat with flames indoors or on a balcony

A barbecue, camping stove or outdoor heater gives off carbon monoxide, which can kill quickly and without warning. Balconies count as indoors for this: fumes come back in, and a fire on a balcony can spread up the building. Fire services say never to barbecue on a balcony. If you have no gas hob, plan for no-cook meals.

If there's a fire

Find out your building's fire strategy now. Your landlord, managing agent or housing provider must be able to tell you. Many high-rise blocks have a stay safe (or "stay put") policy. In Greater Manchester, the fire service says that for those buildings:

  • If the fire is in your flat: get everyone out, close the door behind you, leave the building and call 999.
  • If the fire is somewhere else in the building: you're usually safer staying in your flat, unless heat or smoke is affecting you, you feel unsafe, or firefighters tell you to leave.
  • Use the stairs, never the lift.
  • When you call 999, give the building name, your flat number and which floor you're on.

Some buildings have a different strategy, such as everyone leaving when the alarm sounds. Follow the one for your building, and the instructions of firefighters on the day.

If someone can't use the stairs

Tell your landlord or building manager if anyone in your home would struggle to get down the stairs in an emergency, because of age, disability, illness or a baby and other small children. Ask them to record it and whether they'll make a personal evacuation plan. Tell the fire service when you call 999, too. In England, taller blocks must now offer this if you ask: see Residential PEEPs.

Also join the Priority Services Register. It's free, and it means the power and water companies know about you.

Sort these out this week

  1. Ask your landlord or building manager: what's our fire strategy, and what does the main door do in a power cut?
  2. Store bottled water: 3 litres per person per day, for 3 days. For 2 people that's 18 litres.
  3. Put a head torch and spare batteries by the front door.
  4. Stock 3 days of food that doesn't need cooking or a fridge.
  5. Charge a power bank and keep it charged.
  6. Agree with carers and family how they'll get in if the buzzer doesn't work.
  7. If anyone can't manage the stairs, tell your landlord and join the Priority Services Register.
