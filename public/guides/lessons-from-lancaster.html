When a city lost power for four days: lessons from Lancaster

6 minute read · Last checked 11 November 2026 ·

At 22:45 on Saturday 5 December 2015, Storm Desmond pushed the River Lune into an electricity substation, and Lancaster went dark. Power didn't fully return until 9 December. It's the closest the UK has come to a real test of the government's three-day advice.

What stopped

  • Light and heat, in homes and in care homes.
  • Water, in some places, where supplies relied on electric pumps.
  • Mobile phones, the internet and TV. People walked the streets looking for signal, and there was a long queue at the one working phone box.
  • Money. Card payments stopped, and most cash machines didn't work.
  • Fuel and shops. Petrol pumps need electricity. Food shops threw away stock from their fridges and freezers.
  • Travel. The railway station closed and the traffic lights failed.
  • Schools and the university closed.

Professor Roger Kemp of Lancaster University later described the next morning as "almost like a zombie movie".

What kept working

Two things: old copper-wire landlines, and FM radio. The local station, The Bay, had a generator and one landline, and sent a reporter outside the blackout to phone in news. Professor Kemp's report for the Royal Academy of Engineering concluded that the most reliable source of news was a radio station using 1960s technology.

What's changed since

  • Copper landlines are going. The old phone network, which worked in a power cut, is due to be switched off on 31 January 2027. Home phones will then run through your broadband router, which needs mains power. Ask your provider about a battery backup, especially if anyone uses a care alarm.
  • Radio 4 has left long wave. FM is now the way to hear national advice. The BBC has its own plan to keep FM broadcasting for at least three days in a national blackout, carrying the government's prepared health advice.
  • Three days is the official aim. The BBC reports that the National Energy System Operator's latest advice is that, after a national failure, power could probably be restored in two to three days.
  • Sewers need power too. Lord Harris, chair of the National Preparedness Commission, warned the BBC that without water moving through it, sewage can block and back up. That's why official advice in a national power cut is not to flush.

The lesson in one cupboard

While writing about all this in November 2026, a BBC reporter lost power on his own street. His torch batteries had leaked, his candles were at the back of a cupboard, and he couldn't find his wind-up radio. Most of us are the same.

What Lancaster means for your home

  1. An FM radio with batteries or a wind-up handle, in a place everyone knows. Write your stations' frequencies on it. See which radio to keep.
  2. Torch batteries kept outside the torch, in their packet beside it, so they can't leak. Check them every season.
  3. Some cash at home, about £10 per person, in notes and coins.
  4. 3 days of water, especially if your water relies on a pump or you live high up.
  5. Food that needs no cooking or fridge, because the shops may be shut too.
  6. A plan for keeping in touch without phones. Agree now who will check on whom, and where you'll meet.
  7. A quarter of a tank of fuel, if you drive. Petrol pumps stop too.
  8. Ask about your landline, before the copper network is switched off on 31 January 2027.
