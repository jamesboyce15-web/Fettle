Eating safely when the fridge and cooker are off

5 minute read · Last checked 11 November 2026 ·

When the power goes off, the clock starts on what's in your fridge. Here's the official advice on what's safe, in plain numbers.

Keep the doors shut

A closed fridge stays cold for up to about 4 hours. A full freezer keeps food frozen for about 48 hours, or about 24 hours if it's half full. Every time you open the door, that time gets shorter. Decide what you want before you open it, and be quick.

What to throw away

  • Chilled food should stay below 8°C. If it's been above 8°C for more than 4 hours, throw it away.
  • A cheap fridge thermometer tells you for certain. Without one, if you can't be sure, throw it out.
  • Don't taste food to check it. Food that would make you ill can look and smell fine.
  • If frozen food has thawed, treat it as chilled food. Don't refreeze raw food that has thawed.

Eat things in this order

The UK Chief Medical Officers advise eating the food that spoils fastest first:

  1. Chilled food, and frozen food as it defrosts.
  2. Short-life food such as sandwiches, salads and cooked meats. These are safe for a day or two if they look and smell normal. Raw meat and fish are fine if you can cook them and they look and smell normal.
  3. Fresh food that doesn't need a fridge, like bread, cake, whole fruit and vegetables, and eggs.
  4. Tins and packets last. Food normally kept at room temperature is still safe after its best before date.

Whole fruit and vegetables, hard cheese, yoghurt, butter, pasteurised milk, and long-life milk or juice (even once opened) are safe for up to 7 days if they look and smell normal. Pre-cut fruit and vegetables last 3 to 4 days, and are safe after that if cooked.

In cold weather, you can keep food outside in a clean, dry, sealed plastic or metal container, out of direct sunlight and away from animals.

Who should be extra careful

After 4 hours without power, these people should not eat chilled ready-to-eat food (things like cooked meats, smoked fish, soft cheese, pâté, prepared salads and sandwiches) or leftovers:

  • babies and young children
  • pregnant and breastfeeding women
  • older people
  • anyone with a weakened immune system, for example from cancer treatment

Eat food from tins and packets instead. If there's no clean water for washing, peel fruit and vegetables. People with a severely weakened immune system shouldn't eat raw eggs.

Anyone being sick or with diarrhoea shouldn't prepare food for others. Wipe surfaces and utensils with antibacterial wipes if you can.

If you can cook

  • A gas hob may still work in a power cut, but the electric spark won't. Light it carefully with a long match. If you smell gas, turn it off and call 0800 111 999.
  • Camping stoves and barbecues are for outside only, in a garden or open space. Never use them indoors, in a garage, a tent, or on a balcony. They give off carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell and which can kill.
  • Cook food until it's steaming hot all the way through.

If you can't cook

You don't need hot food to stay well for a few days. Tins, crackers, oats, long-life milk, fruit and peanut butter will do. See six no-cook meals from your cupboard.

Drink enough

Everyone needs to drink at least 2 litres a day, about 8 cups. Store 3 litres per person per day, so there's some for food and washing hands. Children under 2 who aren't breastfed need at least 1 litre a day, about 4 cups, and formula counts towards it. Avoid alcohol, because it dehydrates you. For babies, see feeding a baby when the power or water fails.

Before it happens

  1. Put a fridge thermometer in the fridge (about £3).
  2. Keep your freezer reasonably full. Bottles of tap water fill gaps and help it stay cold.
  3. Keep 3 days of food that needs no cooking or fridge.
  4. If you have a gas hob, keep long matches next to it.
