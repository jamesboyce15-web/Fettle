Keeping warm when the heating stops

5 minute read · Last checked 12 November 2026 ·

Even a gas boiler needs electricity to run. In a winter power cut, the house cools quickly. Cold is dangerous for older people, babies and anyone with a heart or lung condition, so start keeping warm before anyone feels cold.

Make one warm room

  • Choose one room for everyone to spend time in. A smaller room, away from outside walls, holds heat best.
  • Close the door and curtains, and block draughts under the door with a rolled-up towel.
  • Close doors and windows in the rooms you're not using.
  • No curtains? Hang a blanket or sheet over the window.

Keep bodies warm

  • Wear several thin layers rather than one thick one. Add hats, gloves, thick socks and slippers.
  • Use blankets, sleeping bags and duvets, even during the day.
  • Eat regularly and have warm drinks if you can heat water safely.
  • Get up, move around and stretch at least once an hour if you're able.
  • When sitting, keep your feet up off the floor, where the air is coldest.
  • Babies need to be warm, but not hot. Dress them in layers you can adjust. Take hats off indoors and for sleep. If they feel cold, add a layer of clothing or a lightweight blanket, not a duvet or thick blanket. Feel their chest or the back of their neck to check: hands and feet are often cool. Never put a hot water bottle in a cot.

The carbon monoxide danger

When people are cold, they bring in things to heat the house: a barbecue, a camping stove, a patio heater, or a generator. All of these give off carbon monoxide. You can't see, smell or taste it, and it can kill quickly and without warning.

  • Never use barbecues, camping stoves, outdoor heaters or generators indoors, in a garage, or on a balcony.
  • Never use a gas oven or hob to heat the room.
  • Only light a fire indoors in a proper fireplace with a working chimney, or a stove made for it. Burn only the right fuel: never old furniture, pallets, fence panels, rubbish, petrol or lighter fluid.
  • A generator goes outside, at least 6 metres (20 feet) from the house, with the exhaust pointing away from windows and doors.
  • Never leave a fire, heater or generator unattended, and keep a fire extinguisher nearby.
  • Fit a carbon monoxide alarm in any room with a gas, oil, coal or wood appliance.
  • Signs of carbon monoxide poisoning include a headache, dizziness, feeling sick, tiredness and confusion, often in more than one person at once. If you suspect it, stop using the appliance, get into fresh air, and call 0800 111 999 for a gas appliance. Call 999 if someone is struggling to breathe, confused or collapses. Otherwise call 111 for advice, even if you feel better outside.

Signs of hypothermia

The Chief Medical Officers list these signs:

  • shivering
  • cold, dry skin
  • blue or grey skin or lips (on black or brown skin, look at the palms and soles)
  • slurred speech, slow breathing, and tiredness or confusion

A baby with hypothermia may be cold to touch, floppy, unusually quiet and sleepy, and may refuse to feed.

What to do

  1. Get them indoors or somewhere sheltered.
  2. Take off any wet clothes. Wrap them in a blanket, sleeping bag or dry towel, covering their head.
  3. If they're awake, give them something sugary, like chocolate, and a warm drink that isn't alcohol.
  4. Stay with them and keep them talking.
  5. Call 999. Hypothermia is an emergency.
  6. Don't use a hot bath, hot water bottle or heat lamp to warm them, and don't rub their arms, legs, hands or feet.

Sort these out before winter

  1. Keep enough blankets or sleeping bags for everyone.
  2. Keep hats, gloves and warm socks together where you can find them in the dark.
  3. Fit a carbon monoxide alarm if you have any fuel-burning appliance.
  4. Keep a torch and spare batteries in the room you'll use.
  5. Check on older neighbours and relatives when a cold spell is forecast. See checking on someone.
